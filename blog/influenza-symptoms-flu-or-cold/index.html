Flu or cold? Symptoms, when to see a doctor, and the yearly flu vaccine

Flu comes on suddenly and tends to be more severe than a cold. Here is how to recognise it, how to look after yourself at home, and when to see a doctor.

Permai Polyclinics FortunaJalan Penampang, Kota Kinabalu · 22 September 2026 · 8 min read
The short answer

Flu symptoms come on quickly, often within a few hours, and can include a sudden high fever, an aching body, a dry cough, a sore throat, a headache and feeling exhausted. Not everyone with flu has a fever. A cold builds up gradually over a few days and mainly affects the nose and throat. Most people recover on their own within a week. Older people, pregnant women, young children and people with long-term illness are at risk of complications, so they should see a doctor early. Antibiotics do not work on flu.

Why flu comes up so often at the end of the year

Public health medicine specialist Datuk Dr Zainal Ariffin Omar told The Star in December 2025 that flu cases always rise in November and December. The World Health Organization (WHO) says that in the tropics flu can occur all year, with less regular outbreaks. The Epidemiological Week 42/2025 report recorded flu consultations rising to 13,508 patients nationwide, including 1,174 in Sabah. In November 2025 the Health Ministry reported that private homes had the most flu clusters, followed by schools, kindergartens and childcare centres.

Flu symptoms

Flu symptoms can include:

  • a sudden high temperature
  • an aching body
  • feeling tired or exhausted
  • a dry cough
  • a sore throat and a headache
  • difficulty sleeping and loss of appetite

Children can have the same symptoms, and may also have ear pain, sore eyes, swollen glands, or seem less active than usual.

Flu or cold? The quick comparison

The symptoms are similar, so it can be hard or impossible to tell them apart by symptoms alone. A special test can confirm flu. The usual differences:

Flu (influenza)Common cold
How it startsQuickly, often within a few hoursGradually, over a few days
What it affectsMore than just the nose and throatMainly the nose and throat
EnergyToo exhausted to carry on as normalUsually still able to do normal activities
Runny or blocked noseLess commonMore common
ComplicationsCan be serious, including pneumoniaUsually no serious health problems

Influenza A and influenza B

Both cause seasonal outbreaks. Only influenza A is known to have caused pandemics, and the subtypes that routinely circulate in people are A(H1N1) and A(H3N2). Influenza B has no subtypes and generally changes more slowly than influenza A.

In Sabah, a high fever can also be dengue

Dengue can also bring a high fever, a severe headache, pain behind the eyes, joint and muscle pain, nausea, vomiting and a rash. The Health Minister reported 2,866 dengue cases in Sabah up to 13 June 2026, compared with 1,905 in the same period the year before.

The WHO advises people with dengue to use paracetamol for pain and to avoid anti-inflammatory medicines (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and aspirin. So until a doctor has ruled dengue out, do not take ibuprofen or aspirin for fever or pain, because they can raise the risk of bleeding. If a doctor has you on daily aspirin, do not stop it on your own: tell the doctor you have a fever and ask what to do.

Signs of severe dengue often appear after the fever has gone: severe stomach pain, persistent vomiting, fast breathing, bleeding gums or nose, blood in vomit or stools, fatigue, restlessness, being very thirsty, pale and cold skin, or feeling weak. Get care immediately if you have any of these. Read more in our guide to fever in children, dengue and HFMD.

How long is flu contagious?

Flu virus can be detected from the day before symptoms start until 5 to 7 days after you fall ill. The first three days of illness are when you are most contagious. Young children and people with weak immunity can be contagious for longer. According to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), most experts think flu spreads mainly through droplets when people cough, sneeze or talk.

The Health Ministry advises people with symptoms to:

  • avoid public places
  • wear a face mask if they have a cough or cold
  • wash their hands often and follow cough etiquette

Looking after yourself at home

Flu is caused by a virus, so antibiotics will not relieve your symptoms or speed up your recovery. What helps:

  • Rest and sleep.
  • Drink plenty of water to avoid dehydration.
  • Paracetamol for fever and aches, following the label.

A few things to watch when buying cold and flu medicines:

  • Do not take other cough and cold medicines with paracetamol or ibuprofen tablets, because you may take more than you should. Some cold medicines also contain paracetamol.
  • Children under 6 should not use decongestants. Nasal sprays you buy yourself should not be used for more than a week, because they can make symptoms worse.
  • Some cough and cold medicines are not suitable for babies, children and pregnant women. Ask a doctor or pharmacist first.
  • Do not give aspirin to children under 16, and do not give honey to babies under 12 months.
  • There is little evidence that vitamin C, echinacea or garlic prevent colds.

Antiviral medicine

According to the CDC, antiviral medicine can ease symptoms and shorten the illness by about a day. The CDC says it is available only on prescription and works best when started within 1 to 2 days of the first symptoms. It is especially important to start it as soon as possible for people in hospital, people who are very unwell, and people at high risk of complications. Starting later can still help, especially for people at high risk. Most people with mild illness who are not at high risk do not need it. The doctor decides whether it is right for you.

Call 999 or go straight to a hospital emergency department if you have:

  • sudden chest pain
  • severe difficulty breathing, such as gasping, choking or being unable to speak
  • coughing up blood

Do not drive yourself. Ask someone to take you, or call 999 for an ambulance.

Get medical care right away if:

  • Adults: difficulty breathing or shortness of breath, persistent pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen, persistent dizziness, confusion, cannot be woken, seizures, not urinating, severe muscle pain, severe weakness or unsteadiness, or a long-term illness getting worse
  • Children: fast breathing or trouble breathing, bluish lips or face, ribs pulling in with each breath, chest pain, severe muscle pain (the child refuses to walk), no urine for 8 hours, dry mouth, no tears when crying, not alert or not interacting when awake, seizures, or a long-term illness getting worse
  • A child with a fever above 40°C that does not come down with fever medicine, or a baby under 12 weeks with any fever
  • A fever or cough that gets better, then comes back or gets worse

This list is not complete. Get medical advice for any other symptom that is severe or worrying. If you are not sure, or cannot get to hospital safely, call 999.

See a doctor as soon as possible if your child is under 5, you are 65 or over, you are pregnant or have recently given birth, you have a long-term illness such as diabetes or heart, lung, liver, kidney, brain or nerve disease, or you have a weak immune system. The same applies if you feel very unwell, your symptoms have not improved after 7 days, or you are worried about your child.

The flu vaccine: who it is recommended for

The WHO says vaccination is the best way to prevent flu. A yearly vaccine is recommended especially for:

  • pregnant women
  • children aged 6 months to 5 years
  • people over 65
  • people with long-term illness
  • health workers

It takes about two weeks after the injection for your body to make antibodies. You need a new one every year because protection fades over time and the virus keeps changing. Some children need more than one dose, and the doctor can explain the schedule. The flu vaccine cannot give you flu, but you can still catch flu after being vaccinated. Side effects are usually mild and short-lasting, such as soreness or swelling at the injection site, a headache, fever or muscle aches. Serious side effects such as a severe allergic reaction and Guillain-Barré syndrome are rare. Ask your doctor whether the vaccine is right for you.

Questions people ask

How long does the flu last?

Most people recover on their own within a week, but the cough can last two weeks or more. See a doctor if your symptoms have not improved after 7 days.

Is influenza A dangerous?

Most people with flu recover fully within a few days to a week. The Health Ministry says it can cause serious complications in high-risk groups such as children, older people and people with long-term illness, so they are advised to get checked early. In severe cases flu can kill, and serious problems can happen to anyone.

Do antibiotics help with flu?

No. Antibiotics do not work on viral infections such as flu, and they will not relieve your symptoms or speed up your recovery.

Can the flu vaccine give you flu?

No. The flu vaccine cannot cause flu. Side effects such as soreness at the injection site, a headache or fever are usually mild and short-lasting. You can still catch flu after being vaccinated.
