Your child has a fever at night. Is it dengue, HFMD or flu, and when should you see a doctor?

A fever at 2am is scary. Most fevers are the body fighting an infection, and pass in a few days. Here is how to look after your child and when to see a doctor.

Permai Polyclinics FortunaJalan Penampang, Kota Kinabalu · 15 September 2026 · 9 min read
The short answer

A fever is a temperature of 38°C or higher. Most fevers in children come from infections and settle within 1 to 4 days. See a doctor straight away if your child is under 3 months old with any fever, if the fever lasts more than 2 days, or if they are drowsy, not drinking, or have a rash. In Sabah, keep dengue in mind with any high fever: if your child is over 2 months old, use paracetamol only, and avoid ibuprofen until a doctor has ruled dengue out.

First, look at how your child is acting, not only the number

In a child over 6 months, 39°C with playing and drinking is often less worrying than 38°C with a child who is floppy and will not drink. In younger babies the number itself matters, so check the list near the end of this page. A fever is the body's normal way of fighting infection. Watch your child, not only the thermometer.

Dengue: up by half in Sabah this year

Sabah recorded 2,866 dengue cases up to 13 June 2026, 50% more than the same time last year. Across Malaysia there had been 23 dengue deaths by then, up from 16 a year earlier. The Health Minister said Sabah had recorded no deaths at that point. Kota Kinabalu, Tawau, Sandakan, Penampang and Putatan had the most cases in Sabah.

Dengue starts with a sudden high fever that lasts 2 to 7 days, often with headache, pain behind the eyes, body and joint aches, vomiting and sometimes a rash. The dangerous time is usually around when the fever comes down. It often starts after day 3, but it can start earlier or as late as day 8, and some children get warning signs before the fever has gone. Parents can think their child is getting better just as the warning signs appear.

Dengue warning signs. Go to a hospital emergency department straight away if your child has:

  • stomach pain, or vomiting again and again
  • bleeding from the gums or nose, red spots on the skin, blood in vomit, black stools, or heavy periods or vaginal bleeding
  • no energy or poor feeding
  • unusual sleepiness, restlessness and irritability, or a fit
  • cold, pale or clammy skin, hands or feet
  • fast or difficult breathing
  • passing much less urine than usual, or far fewer wet nappies

A dengue blood test (NS1) picks up the virus best in the first days of fever, but a negative result does not always rule dengue out. A full blood count shows the platelet and blood cell levels the doctor watches. A child with dengue who is cared for at home needs a check and a blood test every day until the danger time has passed. Babies with dengue should be cared for in hospital.

HFMD (hand, foot and mouth disease)

HFMD is common in children under 5 and spreads fast in nurseries and kindergartens. In the first months of 2025, Sabah had more than 4,300 cases, and Kota Kinabalu had the most of any district.

  • It starts with a fever, sore throat and a child who refuses food.
  • Painful sores follow inside the mouth, then spots or small blisters on the palms, soles of the feet and the bottom.
  • It spreads through saliva, blister fluid and stools, so wash hands well after nappy changes.
  • Do not take your child to school, nursery or public places while they have symptoms. Keep them home until they have recovered, and ask the doctor or nursery when they can go back. Most children recover in 7 to 10 days.

The most common problem is dehydration, because swallowing hurts. Cold water, milk and soft food help. In a small number of children, one of the viruses (EV71) can affect the brain or heart. Go to hospital if your child stops drinking, has very few wet nappies, is very sleepy, has jerky movements or weak arms or legs, is breathing fast, or keeps vomiting. See a doctor if the fever lasts more than 3 days, or if your baby is under 6 months old.

Flu

Flu brings a sudden fever with cough, runny nose, sore throat and body aches, and children often seem more tired than with a cold. Young children with flu can spread it for longer. A yearly flu shot is the best protection from 6 months old. Read our guide on flu symptoms and the flu shot.

What to do at home tonight

  • Measure properly. A digital thermometer under the arm works for all ages.
  • Paracetamol brings the fever down and makes your child comfortable. Give it if they are distressed or uncomfortable. Use the dose for their age or weight on the label, at least 4 hours apart, and no more than 4 doses in 24 hours. Measure it with the syringe or spoon that comes with it.
  • Babies: do not give paracetamol to a baby under 2 months. Babies aged 2 to 3 months can have it only in limited doses, and only if they were born after 37 weeks and weigh over 4 kg, so ask a doctor first.
  • If dengue is possible, ask a doctor or pharmacist for the right dose, because it may need adjusting to protect the liver. Do not also give cold or flu medicines that contain paracetamol. Get medical advice if you gave too much, or if your child has needed it for more than 3 days.
  • Avoid ibuprofen (Nurofen), mefenamic acid (Ponstan), diclofenac suppositories and other anti-inflammatory painkillers while dengue is possible, because they raise the risk of bleeding. Do not give aspirin to a child under 16 unless a doctor has prescribed it. If a doctor has prescribed any of these for your child, speak to the doctor before the next dose.
  • Give fluids often. Small sips every few minutes: breastfeeds, milk, oral rehydration salts or water. If dengue is possible, do not give plain water alone.
  • Dress them lightly. Keep them in light clothes. Do not add extra layers or wrap a feverish child in blankets.
  • Sponging is not needed. The NHS advises against sponging a child to cool them. The Malaysian dengue guideline allows lukewarm sponging for a high fever. If you try it, use lukewarm water only, and stop if your child is uncomfortable. Do not wipe a child with rubbing alcohol, which can be absorbed through the skin and cause poisoning, and do not give a cold bath.
  • You do not need to wake a sleeping child just to give medicine, but do check on them regularly through the night.

Fever fits (febrile seizures)

Some children between 6 months and 6 years old have a fit when they have a high temperature. It is frightening to watch, but most fits last 2 to 3 minutes and are unlikely to harm your child.

  • Protect their head with your hands or something soft, and move dangerous objects away. Do not move your child or hold them down, and do not put anything in their mouth.
  • Time the fit.
  • When the fit stops, lay your child on their side and make sure nothing in their mouth is blocking their breathing.
  • Call 999 if it is their first fit, if it lasts more than 5 minutes, if they have trouble breathing, if only one side of the body stiffens or twitches, if they have another fit within 24 hours, or if they are more sleepy than usual for more than 1 hour afterwards. Do not drive to hospital while the fit is going on or your child is unconscious.
  • If your child has had fits before and this one stopped quickly, still get medical help as soon as you can.

Call 999 or go to a hospital emergency department if your child:

  • has a fit for the first time, or a fit lasting more than 5 minutes
  • is struggling to breathe, breathing very fast, or making grunting noises
  • has blue, grey, very pale or blotchy skin, or blue, grey or pale lips or tongue
  • is floppy, drowsy or very hard to wake, is not responding as they normally do, or is not interested in feeding or normal activities
  • is extremely agitated, will not stop crying, or seems confused
  • has a rash that does not fade when you press a glass against it
  • has a stiff neck, or is bothered by light
  • has unusually cold hands and feet, or shows any dengue warning sign above

Bring your child to a doctor tonight if they are under 3 months old with a temperature of 38°C or more, or 3 to 6 months old with 39°C or more, if the fever has lasted more than 2 days, if they are drinking much less or have fewer wet nappies, if they have a new rash or ear pain, or if you are worried. You know your child best.

Questions people ask

What temperature is a fever in a child?

38°C or higher. A normal temperature varies a little from child to child, so if it is just under 38°C, keep watching. See a doctor if your child seems unwell, whatever the number.

Should I wake my child to give fever medicine?

No. Sleep helps them recover, but check on them regularly through the night. Give the next dose when they wake if they still have a fever and seem uncomfortable.

Can I give my child ibuprofen for a fever?

Not while dengue is possible, because ibuprofen raises the risk of bleeding. Use paracetamol until a doctor has ruled dengue out. Even then, ibuprofen is not for a baby under 3 months or under 5 kg. Ask a doctor or pharmacist first if your child is dehydrated, or has chickenpox or asthma.

When is a fever too high?

Any fever of 40°C or more needs a doctor, and so does any fever in a baby under 3 months. How your child is acting matters as much as the number.
